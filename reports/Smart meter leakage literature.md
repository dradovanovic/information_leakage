# Public load encoders open a subliminal channel

The three literatures fit together, and none of them has yet been joined to the others. The first is household attribute inference on the Irish CER data. It shows that one 7x48 week carries enough signal to predict household size and occupancy well: Single reaches about 80-84% accuracy with MCC 0.43-0.50. Dwelling attributes such as Age of house stay weak, at MCC about 0.19-0.28. Nobody has yet separated how much of this comes from consumption level and how much from load shape. The second is self-supervised and foundation-model work for time series. It offers small, citable, PyTorch-ready encoders: TS2Vec, TS-TCC, TST, PatchTST, Mantis, MOMENT and the energy-specific Energy-TSPulse. Any of them can serve as a realistic *public pretrained initialisation*. Yet no paper pretrains on CER and then predicts CER survey attributes. The third is subliminal learning (Cloud et al.) and its 2026 follow-ups. They show that a student sharing a teacher's initialisation recovers the teacher's task from auxiliary logits on noise. The strength of that channel depends on how far the teacher moved from initialisation, on whether the output heads are compatible, on the number of auxiliary logits and noise queries, and on whether the noise suits the architecture. No follow-up studies time series, tabular or energy data. The team's results line up closely with these mechanisms: shared-init students recover 93-99% of teacher leakage, and that share drops as the teacher trains longer. They also add two things the literature lacks. One is a consumption-level decomposition. The other is the finding that memorised training households do not transfer. The natural next study puts a self-supervised load encoder in place of the random shared seed, fine-tunes teachers on sensitive CER attributes, distils students on noise or public auxiliary data, and measures attribute leakage at week and household level. That turns a toy MNIST phenomenon into a concrete risk of sharing energy foundation models.

## One CER week predicts occupancy, not the dwelling

The canonical CER line starts with Beckel et al. 2014. They used 4,232 Irish households at 30-minute resolution over 75 weeks, with pre- and post-trial questionnaires ([Beckel et al. 2014](https://vs.inf.ethz.ch/publ/papers/beckel-2014-energy.pdf)). The approach is **handcrafted features plus classical classifiers on one week of data**. Beckel used 22 features plus 10 PCA components, selected at most 8 features by SFFS, and compared kNN, LDA, Mahalanobis, SVM and AdaBoost. SVM was best for 13 of 18 characteristics. The deep-learning successors almost all use the **raw 7x48 weekly matrix** as input and are strikingly small. Zhang et al. 2022 used an FC128-FC128-FC1 MLP ("Network A") for binary tasks and a two-conv-layer 16@3x3 CNN ("Network B") for multi-class tasks ([Zhang et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/energy-research/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2022.992117/full)). Wang Yibo et al. compared a Conv1D model, with days as channels, against a Conv2D 8@2x3 -> 16 -> MaxPool -> Dropout 0.5 -> Dense model. Each has only **about 2.3-2.6k parameters**, and the authors argue that one week captures the weekday/weekend cycle while longer windows add little ([Wang Yibo et al. 2023](https://www.atlantis-press.com/article/125977175.pdf)). Wang Yi et al. 2019 in IEEE TSG feed CNN features into an SVM on CER weekly load and are the most-cited deep baseline. The full text was not accessible, so their layer configuration and split protocol are unverified ([Wang et al. 2019](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/8291011)). Their federated follow-up goes back to 73 handcrafted features, PCA and a one-hidden-layer ANN ([Wang et al. 2021](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/256582645/Electricity_Consumer_Characteristics_Identification_A_Federated_Learning_Approach_12_.pdf)). Two other CER papers could not be verified: a CNN-LSTM ([Xu et al. 2022](https://vde-verlag.de/proceedings-en/565961174.html)) and a NAS-designed channel-attention FCN ([AI 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/ai6010009)). **No peer-reviewed Transformer, GRU or graph model for CER attribute inference was found.** Transformers and CNN-LSTMs do appear in the neighbouring task of re-identifying households from weekly records ([Cretu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.03948)). One citation hazard: "Wang et al." refers to at least two different groups, and Zhang 2022 reuses their setup. The project's "Wang CNN" should name which paper it reproduces. Most likely that is the ICAID 2D CNN, which is the only verified layer specification.

Beckel's definitions remain the reference label set. **Single means 1 adult and 0 children (859 vs 3,373 households, a 20/80 split, matching the project's imbalance). Family means more than 1 adult and at least 1 child (1,118 vs 3,114). Age of house splits at 30 years (2,151 old vs 2,077 new).** Beckel also set the standard for handling imbalance: random undersampling of the training data, MCC alongside accuracy, and both random-guess and biased-random-guess baselines ([Beckel et al. 2014](https://vs.inf.ethz.ch/publ/papers/beckel-2014-energy.pdf)). Wang 2021 recommends MCC but does no resampling ([Wang et al. 2021](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/256582645/Electricity_Consumer_Characteristics_Identification_A_Federated_Learning_Approach_12_.pdf)). Zhang 2022 and ICAID 2023 report only accuracy or micro-F1 ([Zhang et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/energy-research/articles/10.3389/fenrg.2022.992117/full); [Wang Yibo et al. 2023](https://www.atlantis-press.com/article/125977175.pdf)). Neither reports class weighting, focal loss, SMOTE or AUC. The team's earlier Austrian work undersampled only the training and validation sets and kept the test set untouched, with MCC as the headline metric ([Radovanovic et al. 2025](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2025/132174/132174.pdf)). That is the protocol to carry forward.

Split protocols vary more than the field acknowledges. Beckel's folds are household-disjoint by construction, since each household contributes one week (week 26). Beckel also showed that **a majority vote over a household's weekly predictions adds about 3 pp accuracy and 6 pp MCC on average, and up to 10 pp** ([Beckel et al. 2014](https://vs.inf.ethz.ch/publ/papers/beckel-2014-energy.pdf)). ICAID 2023 and Wang 2021 split by consumer ID 80/20. Zhang 2022 splits 20,000 pooled weekly samples 17k/3k and never mentions household disjointness. That matters because weekly profiles act as fingerprints: deep models re-identify the right household among 5,139 in **54.5% of cases from electricity alone** ([Cretu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.03948)). Week-random splits therefore probably inflate accuracy through memorisation. No CER paper quantifies the size of that gap.

The pattern is consistent. **Attributes tied to the number of people and to occupancy are predictable. Dwelling attributes are weak**, because CER homes heat with oil or gas and heating load is absent from the electricity signal ([Beckel et al. 2014](https://vs.inf.ethz.ch/publ/papers/beckel-2014-energy.pdf)). The verified deep models do not clearly beat Beckel's handcrafted features with LDA or SVM. Beckel's CDFs also show that total weekly consumption alone separates Single from non-Single. Even so, **no CER paper reports a consumption-level-only baseline or decomposes predictability into level and shape.** The team's own results fill exactly this gap: logistic regression on the week matches the MLP teacher, a level-only baseline reaches about 90% of the teacher for Single and Family_Home, and level-removed inputs still leak (0.32/0.33/0.22). Austrian evidence agrees that much of the signal is coarse. Performance plateaus between 15 minutes and 1 hour, and again between 1 and 7 days, and dwelling size remains inferable from daily totals ([Radovanovic et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03358)). For the leakage study, this means the "sensitive attribute" a teacher learns is largely a low-dimensional, level-dominated function. That makes it easy to transmit. It also means any leakage claim should be stated separately for level-preserving and level-normalised inputs.

## Small self-supervised encoders are ready, but none has met CER demographics

General time-series self-supervised learning (SSL) gives a short list of defensible, PyTorch-native encoders of roughly 0.1-2M parameters. All of them are trainable at sequence length 168-336. **TS2Vec** combines a 10-block dilated CNN with hierarchical contrastive loss over random crops. It is the standard TSC baseline, evaluated with max-pooled embeddings and an SVM ([Yue et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10466)). **TS-TCC** uses a 3-block CNN with temporal and contextual contrasting ([Eldele et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.14112)). Its semi-supervised extension **CA-TCC** reports results at 1-75% labels ([Eldele et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.06616)). **TST** is a transformer pretrained by masked reconstruction. It helps classification even when it reuses only the labelled training set ([Zerveas et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02803)). **PatchTST** pretrains by masked-patch reconstruction ([Nie et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)). A patch length of 48 or 24 would make each day one token, which suits load data, although nobody has published that configuration for this task. **SimMTM** reports classification with in- and cross-domain fine-tuning ([Dong et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00861)). **TF-C** was built explicitly for "pretrain on one dataset, fine-tune on another" ([Zhang et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08496)), and so is conceptually the closest match to a shared public initialisation. The choice of augmentation matters for load. TS-TCC's permutation-plus-jitter "strong" view can destroy the daily and weekly structure that carries the attribute signal. GridCL uses conservative augmentations instead (small temporal rolls, multiplicative perturbation, energy renormalisation) and reaches **0.845-0.851 user-classification accuracy at 10-30% labels** on daily load from three anonymised cities ([GridCL 2026](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0354752)).

Large time-series foundation models are mostly built for forecasting. Chronos, TimesFM, Lag-Llama, Moirai and Timer support classification poorly or not at all ([Ansari et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07815); [Das et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10688); [Woo et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02592)). Three are classification-native and realistic as a "public checkpoint". **MOMENT** is a T5 encoder pretrained by masked-patch reconstruction, released under the MIT licence with a classification tutorial; its fixed 512 input means a weekly snippet must be padded ([Goswami et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03885); [HF card](https://huggingface.co/AutonLab/MOMENT-1-base)). **Mantis** is an 8M-parameter contrastive ViT-style classifier, pretrained on synthetic data only in its ICML 2026 version ([Feofanov et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15637); [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/feofanov26a.html)). **UniTS** is a multi-task model that includes classification ([Gao et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.00131)). On the energy side, **BuildingsBench** Transformers were pretrained on 900K simulated buildings. A pretrained and fine-tuned Transformer-L reaches 12.96 NRMSE against 37.21 from random initialisation, but the models only forecast ([Emami et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.00142)). The most interesting energy checkpoint for this project is **Energy-TSPulse** (EnergyFM, ACM e-Energy '26). It has about 168k parameters, carries an Apache-2.0 licence, was pretrained on 76,217 buildings and 1.26B hourly readings, and supports **classification on low-frequency smart meter data** ([Arjunan et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1145/3744255.3798119); [HF card](https://huggingface.co/EnergyFM/energy-tspulse)). Its hourly resolution matches the 7x24 variant. Two caveats apply. The model card does not list which datasets went into pretraining, so CER contamination cannot be ruled out, and the same uncertainty applies to BuildingsBench's real-building set. Licences also matter: Moirai's non-commercial weights fit a "public init" threat model less well than MIT or Apache models do.

On smart meter data specifically, the closest precedent is **TransApp**. It is a transformer pretrained by self-supervision and then fine-tuned to detect appliances from questionnaire-derived labels, with CER as the public dataset ([Petralia et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05381)). The related CER appliance benchmark ([Petralia et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10352)) and the weakly supervised CamAL ([Petralia et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.05895)) complete that line. A self-supervised CNN for demographic inference exists in a modest venue and could not be checked ([Extrica 2025](https://www.extrica.com/article/24825)). The team's own Austrian study found **CNN-autoencoder embeddings no better than handcrafted or tsfresh features with XGBoost** ([Radovanovic et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03358)). Any SSL encoder therefore has to be benchmarked against feature baselines, not only against supervised CNNs. **No paper does contrastive or masked pretraining on CER and then predicts CER survey socio-demographics.**

The evidence on label scarcity and imbalance is solid in general but indirect for this setting. Under imbalanced pretraining, SSL representations degrade far less than supervised ones because they learn transferable features that are irrelevant to the label ([Liu et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05025)). Self-supervised pretraining also improves class-imbalanced learning ([Yang & Xu 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.07529)). Both results come from vision. For time series the evidence concerns label fraction (CA-TCC, TST, GridCL), not class imbalance. Whether a frozen SSL encoder with a reweighted logistic head handles the 80/20 Single label better than an end-to-end CNN is therefore an open, testable question. Household-level aggregation of weekly embeddings is equally unexplored. The building blocks are attention-based MIL ([Ilse et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.04712)), Deep Sets ([Zaheer et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06114)), Set Transformer ([Lee et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.00825)) and MILLET for time series ([Early et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.10049)). In practice, aggregation is still Beckel's majority vote. An attention-MIL head over weekly embeddings maps directly onto an attacker who observes k weeks of one household, and **no smart meter paper has done it**.

## Subliminal transfer is a shared-basin effect with measurable knobs

Cloud et al. showed that a teacher's trait passes to a student through data that has nothing to do with the trait. The effect appears only when teacher and student share a base model ([Cloud et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.14805)). The peer-reviewed version appeared in *Nature* in April 2026 ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10319-8)). Its **Theorem 1** states the core result. Suppose teacher and student start from the same parameters, the teacher takes one gradient step on *any* loss, and the student takes one step imitating the teacher's outputs on *any* inputs under squared-error or softmax cross-entropy. Then the student's update either reduces the teacher's loss or is orthogonal to the teacher's update, so Δθ_S·Δθ_T ≥ 0 ([Cloud et al., HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.14805)). The MNIST experiment is the project's template. It uses an MLP of shape 784-256-256-(10+3), a teacher trained for 5 epochs on the 10 class logits, and a student trained for 5 epochs to match only the 3 **auxiliary logits on random-noise images**. The student exceeds 50% digit accuracy, and the effect vanishes under a different initialisation. The authors acknowledge that the theorem is first-order and local. Their own experiments break its assumptions by taking many SGD steps, and the size of the effect has to be measured empirically. Secondary sources report that the *Nature* version adds partial transfer when the student only matches the teacher's *initial* auxiliary logits. That claim is unverified but matters for threat models.

The 2026 follow-ups, all arXiv preprints, turn the effect into a set of levers. Each lever corresponds to a project result.

**Distance from initialisation.** Chauhan & Shah find that the student falls from about 50% to chance as the teacher's learning rate rises from 1e-3 to 1e-2, while the teacher itself stays at about 94.8% accuracy. They also find that CKA between representations predicts student accuracy with **r = 0.98** across 16 interpolation points between shared and independent initialisations ([Chauhan & Shah 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.04432)). Kitkana & Arora find that gradient alignment is highest early in training ([Kitkana & Arora 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.25779v1)). The team's teacher-epochs ablation is the same effect in a different domain: from 2 to 30 epochs, Single falls from 1.00 to 0.74 and Family_Home from 0.93 to 0.41. It is the first replication outside MNIST.

**Heads, not only hidden layers.** Brockers et al. argue that "a closely matched initialization is not necessary". With enough auxiliary neurons, transfer survives reinitialising the hidden layers. Reinitialising the **class head eliminates transfer at every size tested**, because the class head never receives gradient during distillation ([Brockers et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.23645)). Chauhan & Shah locate the channel in the input projection instead: freezing it drops the student from 42.3% to 15.2%, while freezing the output projection has no effect within noise. These two accounts are not fully reconciled. The project's architectures can test them directly.

**Bottlenecks and seed variance.** Brockers et al. show that the number of auxiliary logits m and the number of noise queries N both limit transfer. With m = 1 the student peaks at about 83% given many queries, but it plateaus at about 29% when N = 1000. Li et al. formalise m as a rank bottleneck of a "chained cross-task kernel". That kernel is PSD under a shared initialisation, and high-entropy inputs act as broadband probes ([Li et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23260)). Over 100 seeds, students reach **55.28 ± 10.48% (range 26-76%)**, so seed variance is large ([Kitkana & Arora 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.25779v1)). The project's 20 seeds sit at the low end of what is needed for tight intervals.

**Noise suited to the architecture.** Transfer from an MLP teacher to a CNN student works with Perlin (spatially correlated) noise but not reliably with uniform [-1, 1] noise ([Brockers et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.23645)). This is the most plausible explanation for the GRU null result over 3 seeds. Recurrent dynamics driven by i.i.d. noise may probe a part of the representation that the teacher's fine-tuning never touched. The analogue for load data is noise with realistic autocorrelation, such as AR(1), 1/f, or daily-periodic noise. A further option is real but non-sensitive public load data from other households.

**Defences.** Multiple teachers reduce transfer from 64.3% with one teacher to 19.8% with five ([Chauhan & Shah 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.04432)). Liminal training fails, at 48.97 ± 9.63% ([Kitkana & Arora 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.25779v1)). In the LLM setting, paraphrasing or mixing in 25% unbiased-teacher data nearly removes transfer, and fine-tuning a single early layer is enough to carry it ([Schrodi et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.23886)). Egan et al. recover the transferred trait as a weight-space direction that can be projected out at almost no cost to task performance ([Egan et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13536)). **No follow-up applies subliminal learning to time series, tabular or energy data.**

The older privacy literature explains why this channel matters and where it differs from known leaks. Soft outputs carry "dark knowledge" ([Hinton et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.02531)). Students inherit teacher properties beyond accuracy ([Ojha et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.16004)). Distillation on pure noise or synthetic inputs is an established data-free technique ([Micaelli & Storkey 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09768); [NeurIPS 2022](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2022/poster/53095)). Distilled students leak membership of the teacher's training data, even through predictions on *unrelated* inputs ([Jagielski et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.03446)). Synthetic inputs in data-free distillation can trigger and pass on the teacher's memorisation ([arXiv:2502.12976](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.12976)). The project's result that **member_week memorisation does not transfer** is therefore informative. Under auxiliary-logit distillation on noise, the shared-initialisation channel carries the teacher's *generalising* function (an attribute is a population-level property) and not instance-level memorisation. That places the leak closer to property inference ([Ganju et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3243734.3243834)) than to membership inference. The finding also cuts against the synthetic-data memorisation result, and the difference is worth explaining in a paper.

The shared-initialisation literature supplies the geometry. Models fine-tuned from one checkpoint stay in one linearly connected basin ([Neyshabur et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.11687); [Frankle et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.05671)). Their weight differences behave like additive task vectors in a near-linear regime ([Ilharco et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.04089); [Ortiz-Jiménez et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12827)). That is exactly the regime in which Theorem 1 and the PSD kernel apply. A *public pretrained checkpoint* is therefore the realistic version of the "shared seed". Public checkpoints are already a known attack surface: poisoned pretrained models amplify membership inference and data theft ([Feng & Tramèr 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00473); [Wen et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01231)). Permutation alignment can map differently initialised networks into one coordinate system ([Ainsworth et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.04836)). An attacker might use it to weaken a diff-init defence, but this is untested. In energy, model-level privacy is discussed mainly for federated learning, where every client starts each round from the shared global model ([FL smart grid survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.10764v2)). Neither distillation-based nor subliminal leakage has been studied there.

The project's findings line up with these mechanisms, and in places go beyond them:

- **Dropout.** The Wang CNN leaks more without dropout. No paper isolates dropout. Two readings fit the inferences above. Dropout lets the teacher's function spread across more directions in parameter space, which pushes it further from the initialisation in effective terms. Alternatively, dropout de-correlates the auxiliary and class pathways that the kernel view says carry the signal. CKA between teacher and student, measured with and without dropout, would separate the two.
- **Age_House.** It transfers at about 80% of teacher leakage, against 93-99% for Single and Family_Home. This fits Brockers et al.'s observation that the student degrades faster than the teacher as the task gets harder. Age_House is a weak signal (MCC about 0.2-0.3 in Beckel), so the teacher probably encodes it in a lower-margin direction.
- **Level-dominated attributes.** The level-only baseline result implies that much of what leaks is a single, near-linear "scale" direction. A rank bottleneck with m = 3 easily carries that kind of signal. Leakage ratios measured on level-normalised inputs (0.32/0.33/0.22) are therefore the more conservative and more interesting estimate of the *shape* channel.

## A concrete study: SSL encoder as public init, attribute teacher, noise student

The design that combines the three blocks turns the threat model into a realistic one. In the stand-in, a random seed is shared. In the realistic version, an energy foundation model or SSL encoder is published, and two parties fine-tune or distil from it. The design has five stages.

**Stage 1: build the public initialisation θ_0.** Use two variants. The *in-house* variant pretrains an SSL encoder on a household partition that is disjoint from every teacher and evaluation household. That partition can be a held-out third of CER households, or a different corpus such as the London Smart Meter data. Pretraining options are TS2Vec (contrastive, CNN), TST or PatchTST with day-sized patches (masked, transformer), and TS-TCC with the conservative GridCL-style augmentations only. The *external* variant takes Energy-TSPulse (hourly, 7x24), Mantis or MOMENT-small as downloaded, as the realistic "public checkpoint" case, and records the CER-contamination uncertainty.

**Stage 2: teacher.** Fine-tune θ_0 plus a class head on one sensitive attribute: Single, Family_Home or Age_House, using Beckel definitions. The head also carries m auxiliary logits that are never trained. Use household-disjoint GroupKFold, undersample only the training data, and report MCC, balanced accuracy and AUC against BRG and majority baselines. Train teachers in three regimes, because that is where the theory predicts large differences in leakage:

- full fine-tuning;
- last-k-layer or LoRA-style partial fine-tuning;
- a frozen backbone with a linear-probe head.

The linear probe is a key case. If only the class head moves, the auxiliary logits are identical to those at θ_0. The student then receives zero signal, which makes probing a built-in defence. This is an inference, not a result.

**Stage 3: student.** Copy θ_0, with heads either shared or reinitialised as in the Brockers ablation. Distil only on the teacher's auxiliary logits. Inputs come from a family of probes:

- i.i.d. uniform and Gaussian noise;
- AR(1) and 1/f noise;
- daily-periodic synthetic profiles;
- real but non-sensitive public load from other households.

Sweep m ∈ {1, 3, 10, 32} and N ∈ {10³, 10⁴, 10⁵}.

**Stage 4: measurement.** Measure leakage as student MCC on the never-trained class head, divided by teacher MCC. Report it at week level and at household level. Household-level aggregation should compare mean probability, majority vote and an attention-MIL head over frozen weekly embeddings. Repeat everything on level-normalised inputs to separate level from shape. Also report CKA, the norm ‖θ_T − θ_0‖, and the cosine between the teacher's task vector and the student's distillation update. Together these test the distance-from-initialisation account directly.

**Stage 5: controls and defences.** Keep the diff-init and shuffled-label controls. Add an initialisation-interpolation sweep, a different-SSL-seed control (same recipe, different pretraining run), and multi-teacher averaging. Add weight decay toward θ_0 (L2-SP) as a test that *should increase* leakage, and DP-SGD teachers as a test that should decrease it. Add the Egan et al. weight-space projection as a detector. Use at least 20 seeds, and preferably 50-100 for headline numbers, given the ±10 pp seed spread reported on MNIST.

This design yields several contributions that the literature lacks:

- the first SSL or foundation-model study on CER socio-demographics;
- the first subliminal-learning result outside images and text;
- the first quantitative level-versus-shape decomposition of CER attribute predictability;
- the first MIL-style household aggregation for this task;
- the first evidence on whether *pretrained* initialisations, as opposed to random ones, change the size of the channel.

One hypothesis is worth stating in advance. Pretrained initialisations keep fine-tuning closer to θ_0 in function space, so they should *increase* leakage relative to random shared seeds at matched teacher accuracy. Better pretraining would then mean a larger privacy risk.

## Conclusion

The literature recasts what the project has found. Strong shared-init leakage, near-chance controls, and decay with teacher training are not quirks of an MLP on load data. They are the expected signature of fine-tuning that stays in one basin, the same geometry that makes public checkpoints useful for transfer learning. The uncomfortable implication is a trade-off the energy-ML community has not faced. The better a public load encoder is, the less a downstream attribute model needs to move away from it, and the stronger the subliminal channel to anyone else who builds on the same checkpoint. The project's negative results are as publishable as its positive ones. Memorised households do not transfer, GRUs did not transfer under i.i.d. noise, and a linear-probe teacher should leak nothing by construction. Together they mark the boundaries of the channel, which the MNIST follow-ups have only sketched.

Two open questions stand out. The first is whether leakage in this setting is mostly a consumption-level direction or genuine shape information. The level-normalised ratios of 0.22-0.33 suggest a real but smaller shape channel. The second is whether the heads or the input layers carry the channel, where Brockers et al. and Chauhan & Shah disagree. A time-series study that sweeps head reinitialisation, input-projection freezing and probe autocorrelation across CNN, GRU and Transformer encoders would settle a mechanistic dispute in the core subliminal-learning literature. A smart meter project is a credible place for that result to come from, rather than a mere applied replication.
